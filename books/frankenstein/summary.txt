Front Matter: Author, Introduction and Preface (short points) About Mary Shelley (author note) Born in London in 1797, daughter of William Godwin (social theorist) and Mary Wollstonecraft (leading woman writer, author of feminist works). Her mother died soon after her birth. She was raised by servants, then a stepmother, then in her father's intellectual circle. Met Percy Bysshe Shelley in May 1814 and eloped with him that July. They married in 1816, after the death of Shelley's wife. Frankenstein began in Switzerland in 1816 and was her first and most famous novel. Later works: Valperga (1823), The Last Man (1826), Lodore (1835), Falkner (1837). After Percy's death (1823) she returned to England, raised her son and secured his right to the Shelley family title. She died in 1851. Note: the author note says the book was published in 1816. In fact it was written in 1816 and published in 1818, so use 1818 in essays. Title page and epigraph Full title: Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus. Prometheus stole fire from the gods for humans and was punished. Epigraph from Milton's Paradise Lost: "Did I request thee, Maker, from my clay to mould me Man...?" This is the Creature's complaint to his maker, and it sets up the Adam theme. This edition has an Afterword by Harold Bloom (1965), which you've already read. Author's Introduction (1831 edition) Written in 1831 for a new edition. The publishers asked how she, "then a young girl," came up with "so very hideous an idea." Childhood: she loved writing stories, but even more she loved daydreams ("castles in the air"). She lived partly in Scotland, near Dundee, where her imagination flourished. Husband's influence: Percy urged her to prove herself worthy of her parents and win literary fame. Summer 1816: the group stayed near Geneva, neighbours of Lord Byron, who was writing Childe Harold Canto III. The weather was wet and cold, keeping everyone indoors. The ghost-story contest: they read German ghost stories, and Byron proposed, "We will each write a ghost story." There were four writers: Byron, Percy Shelley, Mary, and Polidori. Byron and Shelley soon gave up, and Polidori's idea went nowhere. Mary struggled for days to think of a story. Ideas about invention: she says invention comes "not out of void, but out of chaos," shaping existing materials, like Columbus and his egg. The spark: she listened to Byron and Shelley discuss the principle of life, Dr. Darwin's experiments (a piece of vermicelli said to move) and galvanism (electricity reviving corpses). Maybe a creature could be put together and given life. The waking dream: that night she saw "the pale student of unhallowed arts" kneeling beside the thing he made. It stirred, and he fled in horror. He woke to find it at his bedside, "opening his curtains," with "yellow, watery, but speculative eyes." This is the origin of Chapter 5. She began the next day with the words "It was on a dreary night of November." Percy urged her to expand it into a full novel. She says the Preface was written entirely by Percy. She calls it her "hideous progeny" and says she has an affection for it because it came from happy days. This is a quiet reference to the grief and loss she felt later. Alterations in 1831: mostly style changes. She says she changed no part of the story, though the 1831 text differs from the 1818 original, so note which edition your course uses. Preface (written by Percy Shelley, 1817) The idea of reanimating life was thought "not of impossible occurrence" by Dr. Darwin and German writers, but the author claims not to take it seriously as science. It is not just a ghost or spectre tale. Its impossible event is a way to explore human passions more fully. Aim: to keep the "truth of the elementary principles of human nature" while inventing new situations. She names Homer, Greek tragedy, Shakespeare and especially Milton as models. Moral aim: to show "the amiableness of domestic affection, and the excellence of universal virtue," and to avoid the weak novels of the day. Disclaimer: the hero's opinions are not the author's own, and no philosophical doctrine should be drawn from the book. Origin: begun near Geneva in 1816, in cold, rainy weather, inspired by German ghost stories. Two friends left on an Alpine trip and forgot theirs, so this tale is the only one finished. Dated "Marlow, September, 1817." Key points for your studies Origins: the novel began as a ghost-story game, but grew into a serious story about creation, guilt and responsibility. Women and creation: Mary was a young woman writing a story about birth, parents and abandonment, after her own mother died giving birth to her. Many critics read the novel through this. Science and the supernatural: galvanism and Darwin's ideas show the book's link to real scientific debates of the time. Percy's role: he encouraged her and wrote the Preface, which distances the book from its hero's views. Think about how much his voice shapes the framing. Two editions: the 1818 and 1831 texts differ. Mary's 1831 introduction reflects her later view, so treat it as hindsight. Shared themes with the novel: dreams, isolation, ambition and the idea of "unhallowed arts." He has just arrived and wants her to know he is safe. She was worried about his plan, but so far nothing bad has happened. He is planning an expedition to the North Pole. The cold wind in the city excites him, because it feels like a taste of where he is heading. He imagines the pole as a beautiful place full of sunshine and wonders, not a frozen wasteland. He hopes to discover new lands, solve the mystery of how a compass needle works, and find a shorter sea route that would help all of humanity. He compares his joy to a child setting off in a little boat with friends. He then explains his background. As a child he read travel books in his uncle's library and dreamed of being a sailor, but his father had forbidden it on his deathbed. Later he fell in love with poetry and tried to become a great poet, but he failed and was heartbroken. Then he inherited money from a cousin and returned to his old dream of exploring. For six years he has trained: he went on whaling trips, put up with cold, hunger and lack of sleep, studied maths, medicine and science at night, and even worked as a ship's officer. A captain once begged him to stay. Walton feels proud and asks Margaret whether he deserves to achieve something great. He admits his hopes go up and down and that he will need to keep other people's spirits up too. He ends by saying he will travel to Archangel in two or three weeks, hire a ship and a crew of whalers, and not sail until June. He doesn't know when he will return: if he succeeds it may take years, and if he fails he will be back soon, or never. He signs off lovingly. Key point for your studies: Walton is ambitious and obsessed with glory and discovery. This sets up the book's main theme: the danger of pursuing knowledge and fame without thinking of the cost. Victor Frankenstein will turn out to be very similar. Time is dragging because he is stuck in the freezing cold waiting to leave. He has hired a ship and is gathering sailors, and the men he has so far seem brave and reliable. The main thing he wants to talk about is his loneliness. He has no friend. When he succeeds, nobody will celebrate with him, and when he is disappointed, nobody will comfort him. He wants a friend who is kind, brave, educated and shares his tastes, someone who could correct his mistakes. He admits he is too eager in action and too impatient with difficulties. He is also embarrassed about his education. He ran wild as a boy, read only travel books, and didn't study languages or proper subjects until too late. At twenty-eight he feels less educated than a fifteen-year-old schoolboy. His daydreams are big, but they lack discipline and balance, and he wishes a friend would guide him without mocking him for being romantic. He then describes two of his crew. His lieutenant is very brave and hungry for success in his career. The ship's master is gentle and fair, and Walton chose him because he hates cruelty on ships. Walton tells the master's story: he was engaged to a Russian girl, but she tearfully confessed she loved a poorer man. The master gave up the marriage, handed his farm and money to the poor rival, and tried to persuade her father to agree to the match. The father refused, so he left the country and only came back once she was married to the man she loved. Walton calls him noble, but regrets that he is uneducated and rather dull. Walton insists he is not having doubts. The winter was harsh, but spring is early, so he may sail sooner than expected. He promises to be careful with other people's lives. He then mentions "The Rime of the Ancient Mariner," a famous poem about a sailor who suffers after killing an albatross. He jokes that he won't kill any albatross, so she shouldn't worry. He confesses he is drawn to the mysterious and marvellous in a way he can't fully understand. He ends by asking her to keep writing and sends his love, in case she never hears from him again. Key points for your studies: Loneliness and the need for friendship is a major theme. Walton wants a companion, and he will soon find one in Victor Frankenstein. The Creature will later suffer from the same loneliness, but far worse. Self-education and lack of guidance: Walton's dangerous ambition partly comes from having no one to guide him. Compare this with Victor later. The Ancient Mariner reference foreshadows a story about a man who breaks natural order and suffers for it. Walton writes in a hurry to tell Margaret he is safe and the voyage is going well. The letter will travel back to England on a merchant ship that is heading home from Archangel, so he is sending it while he can. He is in good spirits. His crew seems brave and determined, and even the floating sheets of ice passing the ship don't scare them. They have already sailed very far north, but it is the middle of summer, and warm southern winds are pushing them quickly toward their goal. Nothing serious has happened so far, just a few strong winds and a small leak, which experienced sailors barely notice. He promises to be careful, calm and sensible, but he is also very confident. He believes success is certain, asking "why not?" and wondering what could possibly stop a determined human will. He admits his heart is carried away as he writes, and then closes the letter quickly, sending his love. Key points for your studies: Overconfidence and ambition: Walton says he will be "prudent," but his language ("what can stop the determined heart and resolved will of man?") shows pride and a belief that humans can conquer nature. This is the kind of attitude that gets Victor into trouble later. Nature as something to be mastered: He calls the sea "untamed yet obedient," as if nature will simply do what he wants. The story will challenge this idea. Irony: Walton insists nothing bad has happened, which makes the danger ahead feel more ominous. The next letter shows how quickly things change. Walton writes to Margaret about a strange event, and says she will probably see him before the letter reaches her. On July 31st the ship was almost trapped by ice and thick fog. When the fog cleared, the crew saw endless plains of ice. Then they spotted something odd: a sledge pulled by dogs, travelling north about half a mile away. The driver looked like a man, but gigantic in size. They watched him through telescopes until he disappeared. The crew were amazed, since they thought they were hundreds of miles from any land. That night the ice broke and freed the ship. The next morning the sailors found a second sledge that had drifted close on a chunk of ice. Only one dog was still alive, but there was a man inside. Unlike the first traveller, he was a European. He spoke English with a foreign accent, and before coming aboard he asked where the ship was heading. Walton found this odd, since the man was close to death. When told they were going to the North Pole, he agreed to come aboard. He was frozen, starving and in terrible shape. The crew warmed him up, fed him soup and gave him brandy. It took two days before he could speak. Walton moved him to his own cabin and cared for him. The stranger seems wild and sometimes almost mad, but when someone is kind to him his face lights up with sweetness. Most of the time he is sad and in despair. When the lieutenant asked why he was on the ice, he said he was seeking "one who fled from me." He called the man he was chasing a "demon." When he heard the crew had seen a sledge the day before, he became eager and kept asking questions. He now wants to watch for the other sledge, but he is too weak, so Walton promised someone would watch for him. Walton says he is beginning to love the stranger like a brother. This answers his earlier complaint about having no friend: he has found one, though a man broken by suffering. Key points for your studies: The mystery begins. We don't yet know who the stranger is (it's Victor Frankenstein) or who the giant is (the Creature). Shelley keeps the reader curious. Walton's wish comes true. In Letter 2 he wanted a friend; now he has one. Notice the parallel: Walton, like Victor, is isolated. Pursuer and pursued. The stranger chases the other sledge, but later we learn the roles are more complicated. "Demon" vs "being." The stranger uses a harsh word. Watch how the Creature is described differently depending on who is speaking. Frame narrative. Walton is writing this down as a journal, which is how we will receive the stranger's story. He admires him and pities him at the same time. The man is gentle, wise, well educated, and speaks with remarkable eloquence. He has recovered a lot and spends his time on deck watching for the other sledge. Even though he is miserable, he still takes a real interest in other people's projects. Walton has told him everything about his expedition. The stranger listened closely and sympathetically, so Walton opened up completely. He said he would gladly sacrifice his fortune, his life and every hope for the expedition. He even said that one man's life or death would be a small price to pay for the knowledge he wants and for the power he would gain over the natural forces that threaten humanity. As Walton spoke, the stranger's face darkened. He covered his eyes, tears ran through his fingers, and he groaned. Then he cried out something like: "Unhappy man! Do you share my madness? Have you drunk of the same intoxicating drink? Let me tell my story, and you will throw the cup away from your lips." Walton's curiosity was strongly aroused, but the stranger needed many hours of rest before he could calm down. When he recovered, he seemed ashamed of letting his emotions overwhelm him and asked Walton about his early life. Walton spoke of wanting a friend. The stranger agreed: people are incomplete, half-made creatures unless a wiser, better friend helps to improve them. He says he once had such a friend, the noblest of people. But he tells Walton that he himself has lost everything and cannot start life again, while Walton still has hope and a future. Walton ends by praising the stranger further. Even in misery, he still feels the beauty of nature deeply. Walton thinks he is like a heavenly spirit with a halo of calm around him. He tries to work out what makes him so special, and decides it is his sharp judgment, his clear understanding of the causes of things, and his beautiful voice. Key points for your studies: The key warning. The stranger's reaction to Walton's ambition is the turning point of the opening. He sees his own mistake in Walton. The "intoxicating draught" and "madness" suggest that the pursuit of knowledge can be like an addiction. Walton's dangerous language. Notice that Walton says one man's life is a small price. This is exactly the cold attitude that Victor will later regret. Friendship as completion. The stranger's idea that people are "half made up" without a friend connects to the book's themes of companionship and isolation, which the Creature will suffer from too. Foreshadowing. The stranger promises to tell his story as a warning. This is the cue for the frame narrative: Walton records Victor's tale, which contains the Creature's tale. Walton's idealising. He describes the stranger as almost angelic ("celestial spirit", "divine wanderer"). This is a bit of an unreliable view, since Walton is lonely and eager to find a hero. He admits he has suffered terrible misfortunes. At first he decided that the memory of them should die with him, but Walton has persuaded him to change his mind. Like Walton, the stranger once searched for knowledge and wisdom, and he hopes that Walton's wishes won't turn out to be "a serpent to sting" him, as his own did. He doesn't know if his story will actually help, but he sees that Walton is on the same path, facing the same dangers that ruined him. He hopes Walton can take a lesson from it, one that guides him if he succeeds and comforts him if he fails. He warns that the story contains marvellous, hard-to-believe events. In ordinary, tame places people might laugh, but in these wild, mysterious regions many things seem possible. He also says the story carries its own proof of truth. Walton is grateful, but doesn't want to make him relive his pain. The stranger replies that sympathy is useless: his fate is almost fulfilled, he is waiting for just one more event, and then he will rest in peace. Nothing can change his destiny, and the story will show why. He promises to begin the next day. Walton decides to write down the story each night in the stranger's own words, or at least make notes if he is busy. He pictures the stranger's voice, shining eyes and thin raised hand, and says the story must be strange and harrowing, like a storm that wrecked a fine ship "thus," meaning the stranger himself. Key points for your studies: The Biblical echo. "A serpent to sting" hints at the Garden of Eden, where forbidden knowledge leads to a fall. The idea of dangerous knowledge is central to the book. Why he tells the story. It is a cautionary tale, meant to warn Walton before he repeats the same mistake. Fate and destiny. The stranger says his fate is fixed ("irrevocably determined"). Think about whether the novel treats events as fate or as the result of choices. Frame narrative confirmed. Walton is recording this story in a manuscript, so everything we read next is filtered through Walton's writing. This raises questions about reliability. The ship metaphor. The "gallant vessel" wrecked by a storm is a metaphor for the stranger himself, a once-great man destroyed. Claim of truth. He says the story has "internal evidence" of truth. Shelley was making a fantastic tale feel believable, a common Gothic technique. Summary of Chapter 1 (plain English) Victor, the stranger, begins telling his story. He was born into a respected family in Geneva, Switzerland. His father was an honest, hard-working public official who married late in life. The marriage came about through a friendship. Victor's father had a close friend, Beaufort, a merchant who lost all his money. Too proud to live in poverty where he had once been important, Beaufort paid his debts and hid in the town of Lucerne with his daughter, Caroline. It took Victor's father ten months to find him. By then Beaufort was sick with grief and unable to work, and Caroline was supporting them by plaiting straw. Beaufort died in her arms, leaving her an orphan with nothing. Victor's father arrived just then, took care of her, and placed her with a relative in Geneva. Two years later, they married. The couple were very devoted. Victor's father treated Caroline with respect and gratitude, protecting her like a delicate plant. They travelled through Italy, Germany and France for her health, and Victor was born in Naples. For years he was their only child, and they raised him with great love, patience and care. He describes his childhood as one smooth path of happiness, guided "by a silken cord." When Victor was about five, the family visited Lake Como. His kind-hearted mother, who often helped the poor, found a very poor peasant family with five children. One girl stood out: she had golden hair, blue eyes and a delicate, angelic look. The peasant woman explained that she was the daughter of a Milanese nobleman and a German mother who died in childbirth. Her father had fought for Italian freedom, and was either dead or in an Austrian prison. His property was taken, so the girl was left with nothing. Victor's mother persuaded the peasants to let her adopt the girl. She was Elizabeth Lavenza. The night before she arrived, Victor's mother joked that she had "a pretty present" for him. Victor, as a child, took this literally and saw Elizabeth as his own to protect and love. They called each other "cousin," and Victor says she was "more than a sister." Key points for your studies: Idealised family. Victor's childhood is almost perfect: loving, protective, moral. Shelley sets up a happy home so the later destruction hurts more. Ask whether his upbringing explains anything about him later. Parental responsibility. Victor says his parents felt a duty to guide him to "happiness or misery." Later, Victor fails this exact duty toward his own creation. Keep this in mind. Women as gentle and passive. Caroline and Elizabeth are described as delicate, angelic and in need of protection. Many critics discuss how Shelley treats gender roles. Elizabeth as a "possession." Victor treats her like a gift that belongs to him. This hints at his possessive nature. Acts of kindness and adoption. Caroline rescues Elizabeth just as Victor's father rescued Caroline. Compare this with Victor's later abandonment of the Creature. Pride. Beaufort's pride causes his downfall, a small version of a theme that grows in the book. They never quarrel, and their different personalities bring them closer. Elizabeth is calm and loves poetry and the beauty of the Swiss mountains. Victor is more intense and wants to understand why things happen. For him the world is a secret he wants to uncover, and discovering nature's hidden laws gives him a joy close to ecstasy. When Victor is about seven, a second son is born (his brother Ernest, though he isn't named here). The family settles in Geneva and at a country house at Belrive, by the lake. Victor prefers a few close friends to crowds, and he bonds with a schoolmate, Henry Clerval, a merchant's son who loves adventure, heroic stories, knights and King Arthur. Victor says his childhood was as happy as anyone's could be, and his parents were kind and gave them freedom rather than ruling by whim. Victor then explains how his fatal interest began. He says it started from small, almost forgotten sources, like a mountain river that swells into a torrent and sweeps away all his hopes. At thirteen, stuck inside an inn on a rainy day, he finds a book by Cornelius Agrippa, an old writer on alchemy and magic. His father glances at it and dismisses it as "sad trash" without explaining why. Victor believes that if his father had explained that this science was outdated, he would have dropped it and perhaps avoided his ruin. Instead he gets all the works of Agrippa, Paracelsus and Albertus Magnus and studies them eagerly. He finds modern science disappointing, since even great scientists seem to know little about nature's deeper causes. The old writers promise more, so he becomes their follower. He seeks the philosopher's stone (which turns metal to gold) and especially the elixir of life, which could cure disease and make people nearly immortal. He imagines the glory of that. He even tries to raise ghosts and devils, and when it fails he blames his own inexperience. At fifteen he sees a violent thunderstorm. Lightning strikes an old oak near the house and shatters it into thin ribbons. A visiting scholar explains electricity and galvanism, which amazes Victor. Suddenly his old authors seem worthless, and he drops them in disgust and turns to mathematics. He calls this change the work of his "guardian angel," a last effort to save him. But he says it failed: destiny was too strong and had already decided his destruction. Key points for your studies: Foreshadowing and fate. Victor keeps saying his ruin was fated. Ask whether he is avoiding responsibility by blaming destiny, his father, or "the stars." Blaming his father. Victor implies that a little parental explanation would have saved him. Compare this to Victor's own failure as a "parent" later. Science versus alchemy. The old magic and the new science meet here. Victor ends up mixing the ambition of alchemy with the methods of modern science. The lightning and the oak. Electricity is a symbol of nature's raw power and links to the later creation of life. Also note the nature imagery: a river becoming a torrent. Contrast with Elizabeth and Clerval. Elizabeth enjoys nature as it is; Clerval cares about people and morality; Victor wants to dissect and master nature. Elizabeth's gentleness "softens" him, which suggests what will be lost when he isolates himself. Why this matters: Victor's childhood curiosity isn't evil. The book asks where healthy curiosity becomes dangerous obsession. It is a critical essay by the scholar Harold Bloom, so treat it as an academic reading of the novel, one useful interpretation rather than the final word. Why the novel still matters. Bloom starts with the modern movie-goer. Every new Frankenstein film shares in a "romantic terror" that is nearly 150 years old. Mary Shelley was only nineteen when she wrote it, the daughter of two radical thinkers (William Godwin and Mary Wollstonecraft) and the wife of the poet Percy Shelley. Most people now use "Frankenstein" as the monster's name, and Bloom says this popular confusion reflects something true: the creator and the creature are two halves of one being. The central idea: the double. Earlier critics said Victor stands for feeling and the Creature for intellect. Bloom thinks this is too neat, since the Creature clearly has real emotions and desires. His own view draws on Romantic poetry (Blake, Shelley, Byron), where a person has a "shadow" or double. Victor is the mind and feelings turned inward on themselves. The Creature is the mind and feelings turned outward, seeking others. The Arctic chase is like Blake's poem about a Spectre following an Emanation, the pursuer and the pursued bound together. The Prometheus theme. The full title is Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus. Prometheus is the Titan who stole fire from the gods for humanity. Romantics saw him as both a heroic benefactor and a dangerous rebel. Bloom links this to Milton's Satan and the Creature's Adam/Satan comparison. Two paradoxes. First, Victor actually succeeded in creating life, and the Creature turns out more imaginative and humane than he is. Victor's tragedy is not that he went too far, but that he failed to love and fled his responsibility. Second, if Victor had been a better "artist," a less hideous creature would never have been rejected. The Creature says the "human senses are insurmountable barriers," and Bloom agrees that appearance is the problem. Victor as a "moral idiot." Bloom calls him this, mockingly, at the moment of creation. The gap between the huge discovery and Victor's petty reasons (making the body larger because small parts are fiddly) is almost comic. When the eye opens he falls from "supreme maker" to a frightened child. Other literary links. The Ancient Mariner. Walton mentions it, and Bloom sees Victor as a Cain-like figure, like the Mariner, cursed by guilt. The Creature's narrative (Chapters 11 to 16) is, in Bloom's view, the book's greatest achievement. He is innocent and sensitive, then ruined by despair. The epigraph from Paradise Lost ("Did I request thee, Maker...") sums up the Creature's complaint. Percy Shelley's poems Mont Blanc and Hymn to Intellectual Beauty, written the same summer, show the same conflict between head and heart. The ending. Bloom sees the novel as a journey from seeking comfort, to seeking revenge, to seeking self-destruction. The Creature's funeral pyre echoes a Byron poem about a lonely fire. Even Victor's last advice against ambition contradicts itself, which Bloom reads as being true to Victor's desire, not his achievement. Key points for your studies: The double (doppelgänger). Many essays on the novel use this idea: Victor and the Creature as two sides of one self. Responsibility, not science. Bloom argues the sin is failing to love and care, not the act of creating. Compare this with readings that treat the novel as a simple warning against science. The Creature as the more human one. This is a major critical position, but you can argue against it, since he also commits murder. Romantic context. Link the novel to Milton, Coleridge, Byron and Shelley, and to ideas of the sublime, nature and consciousness. A critic with a view. Bloom calls the novel "strong" but "flawed" and clumsy in places. You can agree or disagree in an essay. Preface and Percy Shelley. Another critic Bloom mentions suggests the novel pushes back against Godwin's and Shelley's rational optimism. Note how Percy's preface distances itself from the hero's opinions. Themes Dangerous knowledge and ambition. Victor and Walton both chase glory and discovery. Victor warns Walton that the happiest man is the one who "believes his native town to be the world." Compare the biblical Fall and the Prometheus myth. Parental responsibility. Victor abandons his creation at birth. The Creature says: "I ought to be thy Adam, but I am rather the fallen angel." Compare Victor's loving upbringing with the Creature's total neglect. Isolation and loneliness. Walton has no friend, Victor cuts himself off, and the Creature is utterly alone. Each wants companionship, and isolation drives all three to extremes. Nature vs nurture. The Creature begins gentle and kind, and rejection turns him violent: "misery made me a fiend." Shelley suggests society shapes character. Appearance and prejudice. Everyone judges the Creature by his looks. The blind De Lacey treats him kindly, while those who can see him attack him. Revenge. Victor and the Creature end up mirroring each other, each living only to destroy the other. Guilt, secrecy and responsibility. Victor's silence costs Justine's life and leaves everyone he loves unprotected. Injustice. Justine and Safie's father are condemned unfairly, and the law and the Church fail the innocent. Nature and the sublime. Mountains, glaciers and storms inspire awe and reflect the characters' moods. Nature can heal, but it can also overwhelm. Family, friendship and domestic affection. Shelley's Preface says the book shows "the amiableness of domestic affection." Elizabeth, Clerval and the De Laceys show what Victor loses. Science and playing God. Victor creates life without thinking of the consequences. The book reflects debates about galvanism and Dr. Darwin. Gender and women's roles. Women (Caroline, Elizabeth, Justine) are gentle and passive, and several die as victims. Safie is the exception, independent and brave. Critics link this to Wollstonecraft's ideas. 2. Morals and Lessons Seeking knowledge without thinking of the consequences leads to disaster. Creators are responsible for what they create. Judging by appearance is unjust and breeds cruelty. Kindness and companionship can prevent evil, and rejection can cause it. Silence and secrecy can cause as much harm as action. Revenge destroys both sides. Ambition must be balanced by love, family and human duty. Victor's last words warn against ambition, then contradict themselves. Walton's decision to turn back shows a better choice: people's lives matter more than glory. 3. Characters Victor Frankenstein Intelligent, passionate, ambitious, and loving at first. Selfish, proud and cowardly in how he handles the consequences. Blames fate and destiny for his choices. Flaws: obsession, secrecy, refusing responsibility, judging by appearance. The Creature Born innocent, curious and kind, with a love of music, nature and learning. Educated by watching the cottagers and by reading Paradise Lost, Plutarch and Werter. Rejected and abused, he becomes vengeful and kills William, Clerval and Elizabeth, and frames Justine. He is eloquent and self-aware, and he ends in remorse and self-destruction. Robert Walton Ambitious explorer, lonely, idealistic and romantic. A mirror of Victor, but he learns the lesson and turns back. Also the frame narrator, so the story reaches us through him. Elizabeth Lavenza Gentle, loving, loyal and selfless. She defends Justine publicly. Often seen as the "angel of the house," and her death is the Creature's cruellest revenge. Henry Clerval Warm and humane, loves poetry, languages and people. Foil to Victor: he represents the balanced, moral side of knowledge. Justine Moritz Innocent, grateful and gentle. Wrongly executed. Symbol of injustice and of the innocent who pay for Victor's silence. William, Alphonse and Caroline Frankenstein William is innocence destroyed, Alphonse is the caring, rational father, and Caroline is selfless kindness that costs her her life. The De Laceys (De Lacey, Felix, Agatha) and Safie A virtuous, loving family, yet they reject the Creature on sight. Safie is independent and defies her father. Professors Krempe and Waldman Krempe is harsh, Waldman is kind and inspiring. Neither warns Victor about the ethics of his work. 4. Literary Devices Frame narrative (nested story). Walton's letters contain Victor's story, which contains the Creature's. This raises questions of reliability, since every account is filtered. Epistolary form. The opening letters, and the letters from Elizabeth and Alphonse, add realism. First-person narration. Victor and the Creature each tell their own side, creating sympathy and doubt. Foreshadowing. "The Angel of Destruction," the Mariner reference, "I shall be with you on your wedding-night," and Victor speaking of Clerval as if already dead. Dramatic irony. The reader suspects Elizabeth is the target, while Victor thinks he is. Doubles and foils. Victor and the Creature, Victor and Walton, Victor and Clerval, and Safie and the Creature (both outsiders, but treated differently). Allusion. Paradise Lost (Adam, Satan), Prometheus, Coleridge's Ancient Mariner, Wordsworth's "Tintern Abbey," Goethe's Werther. Biblical imagery. The Fall, the serpent, Adam and Eve, Cain. Symbolism. Fire (knowledge, warmth, destruction), light and darkness, ice (isolation), lightning (nature's power), the miniature portrait (love and blame), and the creature's eyes. Pathetic fallacy. Weather and landscape mirror emotion: the rainy night of creation, the storm at William's death, and the glacier meeting. The sublime. Alpine and Arctic landscapes inspire awe and terror. Gothic elements. Graveyards, corpses, storms, a doomed hero, secrets, a double and the supernatural. Imagery and contrast. Warm domestic scenes against cold, bleak settings, and spring and summer against winter. Irony. The "beautiful" features turn out hideous, a "good spirit" is hunted, and the kindest act (saving a girl) earns a bullet. Hyperbole and emotive language. Words like "fiend," "wretch," "demon" and "monster" show Victor's bias. Parallelism and structure. Justine's trial mirrors Victor's arrest, Victor's isolation mirrors the Creature's, and the ending returns to the Arctic where the frame began. Tone and mood. Anxious, tragic, melancholic and ominous. Open ending. The Creature vanishes into the dark, leaving the questions unresolved. 5. Context to Remember Written in 1816, published 1818 (revised 1831). Check which edition your course uses. Romanticism: feeling, nature, the individual and the sublime. Enlightenment and science: galvanism, Darwin's experiments, and the question of the "principle of life." Mary Shelley's life: her mother died after her birth, her parents were radical thinkers, and she lost children and later her husband. Bloom's reading: Victor and the Creature as two halves of one self, and the Creature as "more human" than his creator. 6. Possible Essay Angles Who is the real monster, Victor or the Creature? Is the book a warning against science, or a warning against irresponsibility? How does the nested narrative affect whom we trust? Is Victor a tragic hero or a selfish one? How does Shelley use nature to reflect emotion? Does Walton's ending offer hope?
